
# Against Baptismal Regeneration: A Biblical and Exegetical Response

---

## Part I — The Positive Biblical Case: Regeneration by the Spirit Through Faith Alone

Before examining the individual proof-texts cited in favour of baptismal regeneration, it is essential to establish what Scripture *clearly and didactically* teaches about the instrument of regeneration and justification. The consistent testimony of the New Testament is that sinners are born again by the Holy Spirit and justified through faith alone — apart from any external rite.

### A. Regeneration is the sovereign work of the Spirit, not of water

**John 1:12–13**
> But to all who did receive him, who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God, who were born, not of blood nor of the will of the flesh nor of the will of man, but of God.

The instrument by which one becomes a child of God is *receiving* and *believing* — and the cause of the new birth is God Himself. Water baptism is conspicuously absent.

**John 3:6–8**
> That which is born of the flesh is flesh, and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit. Do not marvel that I said to you, "You must be born again." The wind blows where it wishes, and you hear its sound, but you do not know where it comes from or where it goes. So it is with everyone who is born of the Spirit.

Jesus defines regeneration as a sovereign, invisible work of the Spirit — likened to wind, not to water administered by a priest.

**1 Peter 1:23**
> Since you have been born again, not of perishable seed but of imperishable, through the living and abiding word of God.

The instrument of the new birth is *the word of God*, not the water of baptism.

**James 1:18**
> Of his own will he brought us forth by the word of truth, that we should be a kind of firstfruits of his creatures.

Again, God brings forth spiritual life *by the word of truth*.

### B. Justification and salvation are through faith — not faith plus baptism

**John 3:16**
> For God so loved the world, that he gave his only Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life.

**John 3:36**
> Whoever believes in the Son has eternal life; whoever does not obey the Son shall not see life, but the wrath of God remains on him.

**John 5:24**
> Truly, truly, I say to you, whoever hears my word and believes him who sent me has eternal life. He does not come into judgment, but has passed from death to life.

**John 6:47**
> Truly, truly, I say to you, whoever believes has eternal life.

**Romans 3:28**
> For we hold that one is justified by faith apart from works of the law.

**Romans 4:5**
> And to the one who does not work but believes in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is counted as righteousness.

**Romans 5:1**
> Therefore, since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ.

**Ephesians 2:8–9**
> For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast.

The consistent pattern is: *believes → has eternal life / is justified*. Nowhere does a didactic salvation text insert baptism as a condition between faith and life.

### C. The case of the thief on the cross

**Luke 23:42–43**
> And he said, "Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom." And he said to him, "Truly, I say to you, today you will be with me in paradise."

The thief believed and was saved — without baptism. If water baptism were the instrument of regeneration, Christ could not have pronounced this assurance.

### D. Cornelius and his household: faith before baptism

**Acts 10:43–47**
> To him all the prophets bear witness that everyone who believes in him receives forgiveness of sins through his name." While Peter was still saying these things, the Holy Spirit fell on all who heard the word… Then Peter declared, "Can anyone withhold water for baptizing these people, who have received the Holy Spirit just as we have?"

Cornelius and his household *received the Holy Spirit* — the definitive mark of regeneration (Rom 8:9) — *before* water baptism. Peter then commands baptism as a consequence, not a cause, of their new life.

**Acts 15:9**
> And he made no distinction between us and them, having cleansed their hearts by faith.

Peter's own retrospective on the Cornelius event: hearts were cleansed *by faith*, not by baptism.

## Part II — Examination of Proof-Texts for Baptismal Regeneration

---

### 1. John 3:5

> Jesus answered, "Truly, truly, I say to you, unless one is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God."

**The claim:** "Water" refers to baptismal water; therefore baptism is necessary for regeneration.

**Response:**

**(a) The Old Testament background controls the meaning.** Jesus is speaking to Nicodemus, "the teacher of Israel" (v. 10), and rebukes him for not understanding *these things* — implying that Nicodemus should have known them from the Old Testament. The decisive background is **Ezekiel 36:25–27**:

> I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean from all your uncleannesses… And I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you… And I will put my Spirit within you.

In Ezekiel, "water" and "Spirit" are both metaphors for God's cleansing and renewing work — not a reference to a baptismal rite that did not yet exist.

**(b) "Water and Spirit" is a hendiadys or epexegetical pairing.** The Greek construction *ἐξ ὕδατος καὶ πνεύματος* (ex hydatos kai pneumatos) governed by a single preposition (*ἐκ*) and joined by *καί* can be read as "water, even the Spirit" — that is, the cleansing water *is* the Spirit. Jesus Himself uses "water" as a metaphor for the Spirit elsewhere (John 4:14; 7:37–39).

**(c) The immediate context defines regeneration as the Spirit's work alone.** In the very next verses (vv. 6–8), Jesus explains the new birth entirely in terms of the Spirit, with no further mention of water. If water baptism were essential, this omission is inexplicable.

**(d) If "water" meant Christian baptism, Nicodemus could not have been expected to understand it.** Christian baptism had not yet been instituted.

---

### 2. Mark 16:16

> Whoever believes and is baptized will be saved, but whoever does not believe will be condemned.

**The claim:** Belief and baptism are jointly necessary for salvation.

**Response:**

**(a) Textual-critical caution.** Mark 16:9–20 (the "longer ending") is absent from the two earliest and most reliable Greek manuscripts (Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus), and many scholars — including conservative ones — regard it as a later addition. Building a doctrine on textually disputed material is exegetically precarious.

**(b) Even taking the verse at face value, the negative clause is decisive.** The second half says: "whoever does not *believe* will be condemned." It does *not* say "whoever is not *baptized* will be condemned." The omission of baptism from the condemnation clause shows that faith is the decisive factor. Baptism is the normal *accompaniment* of faith in the New Testament, but it is faith — not baptism — whose absence brings condemnation.

**(c) Parallel logic.** "Whoever dresses and goes to work gets paid, but whoever does not go to work does not get paid." Dressing is assumed to accompany going to work, but going to work — not dressing — is what earns the pay.

---

### 3. Acts 2:38

> And Peter said to them, "Repent and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins, and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit."

**The claim:** Baptism is "for" (*eis*) the forgiveness of sins — therefore it causes forgiveness.

**Response:**

**(a) The Greek preposition *eis* does not necessarily mean "in order to obtain."** *Eis* has a wide semantic range. In Matthew 12:41, the people of Nineveh repented *eis* (at/because of) the preaching of Jonah — they did not repent "in order to obtain" Jonah's preaching. Similarly, *eis* in Acts 2:38 can mean "because of" or "with a view to" the forgiveness already granted through repentance.

**(b) The grammar distinguishes repentance from baptism.** "Repent" (*μετανοήσατε*) is second person plural; "be baptized" (*βαπτισθήτω*) switches to third person singular ("each one of you"). This grammatical shift suggests that "for the forgiveness of your sins" connects back to "repent" (the main command), not to "be baptized" (the parenthetical individual instruction).

**(c) Peter himself interprets his own words in later speeches.** In Acts 3:19, Peter says: "Repent therefore, and turn back, that your sins may be blotted out." Here, forgiveness follows repentance — with no mention of baptism. In Acts 10:43: "everyone who believes in him receives forgiveness of sins through his name." In Acts 15:9: God "cleansed their hearts by faith." Peter's own consistent teaching is: repentance and faith → forgiveness.

---

### 4. Acts 22:16

> And now why do you wait? Rise and be baptized and wash away your sins, calling on his name.

**The claim:** Baptism washes away sins; therefore baptism regenerates.

**Response:**

**(a) The participle "calling on his name" (*ἐπικαλεσάμενος*) is the key.** In Greek, the aorist middle participle *ἐπικαλεσάμενος* ("calling on his name") functions as a participle of means: "wash away your sins *by calling on his name*." The washing is accomplished through the invocation of Christ's name (i.e., faith expressed in confession), and baptism is the outward occasion/sign at which this occurs.

**(b) "Calling on the name of the Lord" is consistently linked to faith, not to baptism.** Romans 10:13–14: "Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved." Paul then defines "calling" in terms of *believing*: "How then will they call on him in whom they have not believed?"

**(c) Paul's own testimony confirms this.** Paul recounts his conversion in Acts 9 and Acts 26. In Acts 26:18, the Lord tells Paul his mission is so that people "may receive forgiveness of sins and a place among those who are sanctified *by faith in me*." Paul never attributes his own forgiveness to the baptismal water.

---

### 5. Romans 6:3–4

> Do you not know that all of us who have been baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death? We were buried therefore with him by baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life.

**The claim:** Baptism unites us with Christ's death and resurrection — therefore baptism effects regeneration.

**Response:**

**(a) The context is about the *reality* of union with Christ, not the *means* of union.** Romans 6:1–2 poses the question: "Are we to continue in sin that grace may abound?" Paul's answer is that believers have *died to sin*. He then appeals to their baptism as a picture and public declaration of this death — not as the cause of it.

**(b) Paul teaches elsewhere that union with Christ is by faith.** Ephesians 3:17: "so that Christ may dwell in your hearts *through faith*." Galatians 2:20: "the life I now live in the flesh I live *by faith* in the Son of God." The instrumental cause of union is faith; baptism is the outward sign and seal.

**(c) "Baptized into Christ" can refer to Spirit baptism.** 1 Corinthians 12:13: "For in one Spirit we were all baptized into one body." The baptism that actually unites us to Christ is the Spirit's work, of which water baptism is the visible symbol.

**(d) If water baptism itself effected death to sin, all baptized persons would be dead to sin** — yet Paul writes Romans 6 precisely because some baptized people were *not* living as though dead to sin. This shows that baptism pictures a spiritual reality but does not automatically produce it.

---

### 6. Galatians 3:27

> For as many of you as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ.

**The claim:** Putting on Christ happens through the act of baptism.

**Response:**

**(a) Verse 26 is the controlling statement.** Galatians 3:26: "for in Christ Jesus you are all sons of God, *through faith*." Verse 27 begins with *γάρ* ("for"), making it an explanation of verse 26 — not an independent statement. The means of becoming sons of God is *faith* (v. 26); baptism (v. 27) is the public, visible expression of that faith-union.

**(b) The broader argument of Galatians rules out any ceremony as the instrument of justification.** Paul's entire letter argues against adding any external requirement to faith for justification (Gal 2:16, 21; 3:2–3, 11; 5:4–6). Inserting water baptism as a necessary instrument of salvation would contradict the very thesis of the epistle.

**(c) "Put on Christ" is metaphorical language for identification.** The imagery is of putting on a garment — publicly identifying with Christ. Baptism is the occasion of this public identification, but faith is the instrument by which one actually comes to be "in Christ."

---

### 7. Colossians 2:11–12

> In him also you were circumcised with a circumcision made without hands, by putting off the body of the flesh, by the circumcision of Christ, having been buried with him in baptism, in which you were also raised with him through faith in the powerful working of God, who raised him from the dead.

**The claim:** Baptism is the New Covenant equivalent of circumcision and effects spiritual rebirth.

**Response:**

**(a) Paul explicitly states the instrument: "through faith" (*διὰ τῆς πίστεως*).** Verse 12 says believers were raised with Christ "through faith in the powerful working of God." The phrase *through faith* is the instrumental modifier — faith, not the water, is what connects the believer to Christ's resurrection power.

**(b) The "circumcision made without hands" is not water baptism — it is the Spirit's internal work.** Paul distinguishes the spiritual circumcision ("without hands," i.e., not a physical rite) from the outward sign. The "putting off the body of the flesh" is regeneration — accomplished by Christ ("the circumcision of Christ"), not by a human administrator of water.

**(c) Baptism is mentioned as the *sign* that corresponds to this inward reality,** just as circumcision was the sign of the Abrahamic covenant but did not itself produce the faith that justified Abraham (Rom 4:9–12). Abraham was justified *before* circumcision; believers are regenerated *before* (or apart from) baptism.

---

### 8. Ephesians 5:25–26

> Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her, that he might sanctify her, having cleansed her by the washing of water with the word.

**The claim:** The "washing of water" is baptism, which cleanses the church.

**Response:**

**(a) The qualifying phrase "with the word" (*ἐν ῥήματι*) is decisive.** The cleansing agent is not bare water but the *word* — the gospel. This aligns with John 15:3: "Already you are clean *because of the word* that I have spoken to you," and John 17:17: "Sanctify them in the truth; your word is truth."

**(b) If this referred to baptismal water as the cleansing agent, Paul would be contradicting himself.** In Ephesians 2:8–9, written in the *same letter*, Paul has just said salvation is "through faith… not a result of works." Water baptism is something a person *does* (or has done to them); if it were the instrument of cleansing, it would be a "work" in tension with Paul's own soteriology.

**(c) "Washing" (*λουτρόν*) is metaphorical.** The same word appears in Titus 3:5 ("washing of regeneration"), where it is explicitly attributed to the *Holy Spirit*, not to water. The "washing of water with the word" is best understood as the spiritual cleansing accomplished by the word of the gospel, applied by the Spirit.

---

### 9. Titus 3:5

> He saved us, not because of works done by us in righteousness, but according to his own mercy, by the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit.

**The claim:** The "washing of regeneration" refers to baptism.

**Response:**

**(a) The text explicitly excludes works: "not because of works done by us in righteousness."** If baptism — an outward act performed by or upon a person — is what "the washing of regeneration" means, then Paul contradicts himself within the same sentence.

**(b) "Washing of regeneration *and* renewal of the Holy Spirit" is a hendiadys.** The *καί* ("and") links "washing of regeneration" and "renewal of the Holy Spirit" as two descriptions of the same reality: the regenerating, renewing work of the Spirit. The "washing" *is* the Spirit's renewal — not a water rite followed by a separate spiritual renewal.

**(c) The genitive "of regeneration" (*παλιγγενεσίας*) identifies the *kind* of washing.** It is not a washing with physical water but a washing that consists in being regenerated — that is, a spiritual washing. Compare "the circumcision of Christ" in Colossians 2:11, which is not a physical circumcision but a spiritual one.

**(d) Paul's broader theology consistently attributes cleansing to the Spirit and the word.** 1 Corinthians 6:11: "you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and *by the Spirit of our God*." The washing is the Spirit's work.

---

### 10. 1 Peter 3:21

> Baptism, which corresponds to this, now saves you, not as a removal of dirt from the body but as an appeal to God for a good conscience, through the resurrection of Jesus Christ.

**The claim:** Peter says "baptism now saves you" — therefore baptism is necessary for salvation.

**Response:**

**(a) Peter immediately qualifies his own statement.** The moment he says "baptism… saves you," he adds: "*not* as a removal of dirt from the body." That is, it is not the physical application of water to the body that saves. This qualification explicitly rules out the idea that the water itself has saving power.

**(b) What saves is "an appeal to God for a good conscience" (*ἐπερώτημα… εἰς θεόν*).** The Greek *ἐπερώτημα* can mean "appeal," "pledge," or "answer." The saving element is the inward, faith-driven commitment or appeal to God — the heart's cry for a good conscience. This is faith expressed in baptism, not the water itself.

**(c) The saving power comes "through the resurrection of Jesus Christ."** The ground of salvation is Christ's resurrection, not the baptismal water. Baptism *pictures* the resurrection (as v. 20 pictures the flood as a type of judgment and deliverance), but the reality to which it points — Christ's resurrection and the believer's faith-union with Him — is what actually saves.

**(d) The typological context confirms this.** Peter says Noah and his family were "brought safely through water" (v. 20). The water was the instrument of *judgment* on the world; Noah was saved *through* (i.e., safely through) the water by being in the ark. The ark, not the water, saved Noah. Likewise, being *in Christ* (by faith), not the baptismal water, saves the believer.

---

## Conclusion

The consistent testimony of Scripture is that regeneration is the sovereign work of the Holy Spirit (John 3:6–8), effected through the word of God (1 Pet 1:23; Jas 1:18), received by faith alone (John 1:12–13; Eph 2:8–9; Rom 3:28; 4:5), and sealed by the Spirit (Eph 1:13–14) — not by water baptism. Every supposed proof-text for baptismal regeneration, when examined in its immediate context, its grammatical structure, and in light of the whole counsel of Scripture, either (a) uses "water" or "washing" as a metaphor for the Spirit's cleansing work, (b) speaks of baptism as the outward sign and occasion of a salvation already received by faith, or (c) explicitly qualifies itself to exclude the physical act as the saving instrument.

Baptism is a precious ordinance commanded by Christ (Matt 28:19), a public confession of faith, and a beautiful picture of the believer's union with Christ in His death and resurrection. But it is the *sign*, not the *thing signified*. To confuse the sign with the reality is to obscure the gospel of grace and to place a human work where only the sovereign, free grace of God in Christ belongs.

> For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast. (Ephesians 2:8–9)
